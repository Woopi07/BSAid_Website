## 1 · Accounting

### What is accounting?

- **Accounting** is the process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarizing, analyzing, and communicating financial information to users for decision-making.

### Main functions of accounting

- **Identify** — determine which events are accounting transactions.
- **Measure** — assign monetary values.
- **Record** — journalize transactions.
- **Classify** — group transactions into accounts.
- **Summarize** — prepare financial statements.
- **Interpret / Analyze** — understand what the information means.
- **Communicate** — provide information to users.

### Users of accounting information

| Users | What they use it for | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Internal users** | Planning, controlling operations, and making business decisions. | Management, Owners, Employees |
| **External users** | Deciding whether to provide resources to the business. | Investors, Creditors, Government, Customers, Suppliers |

### Accounting equation

In order to better understand the concept of accounting, one needs to grasp the concept of the Accounting Equation first. The basic accounting equation is:

<p class="note-formula">Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity</p>

- This equation is the foundation of every journal entry and, therefore, of the Financial Statements. The Accounting Equation is the product of the **Double-Entry Bookkeeping System**: in every transaction there are always two or more accounts affected, and there is always a debit and a credit. That is why every journal entry has at least one debit and one credit.

## 2 · Elements of Financial Statements

### Assets

- A **resource controlled by the enterprise as a result of past events** and from which **future economic benefits** are expected to flow to the enterprise.
  - *Resource controlled by the enterprise* — control means having the right to use and dispose of the resources. Control is also evidenced by the right to restrict others from using the asset.
  - *Result of past events* — there must be a past transaction that gives birth to the asset.
  - *Future economic benefits are expected* — to be an asset, a resource must have a future benefit or use to the entity.

#### Current assets

An asset is **current** if it is held for the purpose of being traded, or expected to be realized or consumed within 12 months after the end of the reporting period or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. In classifying whether an asset is current or not, first analyze whether the asset is held for trading.

- **Trade assets** — assets that arise from or are used in the normal operation of the business. *Trade assets are usually current assets.*
  - **Cash** — normally current, but if restricted for non-current purposes it becomes non-current. Example: cash restricted to purchase machinery — since machinery is non-current, the cash is restricted for a non-current purpose, making it non-current as well.
  - **Inventory** — merchandise for sale of the entity.
  - **Accounts receivable** — receivables resulting from selling merchandise or services.
  - **Notes receivable** — receivables with a written note promising to pay.
  - **Prepaid expense** — advance payment for services or merchandise to be received in the future.
  - To be classified as current, a **trade asset** must be collectible within **one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer**.
    - *Operating cycle* — the time from purchasing inventory to collecting receivables. If silent, the operating cycle is one year.

> **Sample problem**
> The entity has a note receivable collectible after 4 years. The entity constructs bridges and has an operating cycle of 5 years.
>
> **Analysis:** 1 year vs. an operating cycle of 5 years — the operating cycle is longer, so 5 years is the benchmark for trade assets. Since the receivable is a trade asset collectible inside the operating cycle (4 years is within 5 years), it is classified as a **current asset**.
>
> *Note: The operating cycle can only be used as a benchmark if the asset is a trade asset, such as accounts receivable and notes receivable.*

- **Non-trade assets** — assets that don't meet the criteria of trade assets. *Non-trade assets are usually non-current assets.*
  - **Property, plant and equipment** — tangible assets held for use in the production or supply of goods or services.
  - **Intangible assets** — identifiable, non-monetary assets without physical substance.
  - **Sinking fund** — cash set aside for a non-current purpose, such as buying equipment or paying long-term debt.
  - To be classified as current, a **non-trade asset** must be collectible **within one year**.

> **Sample problem**
> The entity has a mortgage receivable collectible after 4 years. The entity constructs bridges and has an operating cycle of 5 years.
>
> **Analysis:** A mortgage receivable does not arise from normal operations, so the 5-year operating cycle is ignored and only 1 year is used as the benchmark. Since 4 years is outside the 1-year benchmark, the mortgage receivable is a **non-current asset**.
>
> *Note: 1 year is the benchmark if the asset does not arise from the normal operation of the business, such as a mortgage receivable.*

#### Non-current assets

If an asset doesn't meet the criteria of a current asset, it is **non-current**.

- **Trade assets** — non-current if the maturity is more than the operating cycle or 1 year, whichever is longer.
- **Non-trade assets** — non-current if the maturity is more than 1 year.

### Liabilities

- A **present obligation** of the entity **arising from past events**, the settlement of which is expected to result in an **outflow of economic resources** embodying economic benefits.
  - *Present obligation* — the entity is required to pay it.
  - *Arising from past events* — there is a transaction that gives birth to the liability.
  - *Obligating event* — the transaction that gives birth to the liability.
  - *Settlement is expected to result in an outflow of economic resources* — an asset is required as payment.

#### Current liabilities

A liability is **current** if it is due within 12 months after the end of the balance sheet date — in other words, it is expected to be paid in the next year. If the company's normal operating cycle is longer than 12 months, a liability arising from normal operations is current if it is due within the operating cycle.

- **Trade payables** — liabilities incurred as a result of the normal operation of the business. *Trade payables are usually current liabilities.*
  - **Accounts payable** — incurred when buying from suppliers of goods.
  - **Taxes payable** — payable to the government. All taxes are current liabilities except the Deferred Tax Liability, which is non-current.
  - **Salaries payable**
  - **Notes payable** — payables with a written promise to pay.
  - **Provision** — an estimated liability with uncertain timing and amount, e.g. provision for a lawsuit, warranty liability.
  - To be classified as current, a **trade payable** must be expected to be paid within **one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer**.

> **Sample problem**
> The entity has a note payable of ₱4,000,000 with a maturity of 4 years. The operating cycle of the entity is 5 years.
>
> **Analysis:** A note payable is a trade payable, so the operating cycle is used. The 5-year operating cycle is longer than one year, so 5 years is the threshold. Since the 4-year maturity is within the 5-year operating cycle, the note payable is a **current liability**.

- **Non-trade payables** — liabilities that don't meet the criteria of trade payables. *Non-trade payables are usually non-current liabilities.*
  - **Mortgage payable** — payables with real property as collateral.
  - **Bonds payable** — payables evidenced by a bond certificate.

#### Non-current liabilities

If a liability doesn't meet the criteria of a current liability, it is **non-current**.

- **Trade payables** — non-current if expected to be paid after more than the operating cycle or 1 year, whichever is longer.
- **Non-trade payables** — non-current if expected to be paid after more than 1 year.

### Expense

- A **decrease in economic benefit** or **incurrence of liability** that results in a **decrease of equity**, other than withdrawals of owners. Usually incurred to generate income.
  - *Decrease in economic benefit* — decrease in assets.
  - *Incurrence of liability* — increase in liabilities.
  - *Results in a decrease of equity* — there must be an adverse or negative effect on the entity.
  - *Other than withdrawals of the owner* — the decrease in assets or increase in liabilities must result from outside transactions.

The definition of expenses encompasses expenses and losses.

| | Expenses and losses |
| --- | --- |
| **Expenses** | Arise in the course of the ordinary activities of the enterprise. They are generally classified as cost of services rendered or cost of goods sold, distribution or selling expenses, administrative expenses, or other operating expenses. |
| **Losses** | Other items that meet the definition of expenses and may or may not arise in the course of ordinary activities. Losses are decreases in economic benefits and are no different in nature from expenses. |

#### Typical account titles used

- **Cost of Sales** — the cost incurred to purchase or produce the products sold to customers during the period; also called cost of goods sold.
- **Salaries or Wages Expense** — all payments resulting from an employer–employee relationship, such as salaries or wages, 13th month pay, cost of living allowance, overtime pay, hazard pay, holiday pay and other benefits.
- **Telecommunications, Electricity, Fuel and Water Expense** — expenses for the use of telecommunication facilities, electricity, fuel and water. Also called **Utilities Expense**.
- **Rent Expense** — rentals for space, equipment or other assets used in the business.
- **Supplies Expense** — the cost of using supplies (e.g. office or store supplies) in operations.

### Income

- An **increase in economic benefit** or **decrease in liability** that results in an **increase of equity**, other than investments of the owners. Usually earned as a result of incurring expenses.
  - *Increase in economic benefit* — increase in assets.
  - *Results in an increase of equity* — there must be a positive effect on the entity.
  - *Other than investments of the owners* — the increase in assets or decrease in liabilities must result from outside transactions.

The definition of income encompasses revenue and gains.

| | Revenue and gains |
| --- | --- |
| **Revenue** | Arises in the course of the ordinary activities of an enterprise and goes by a variety of names, including sales, fees, etc. |
| **Gains** | Other items that meet the definition of income and may or may not arise in the course of ordinary activities. Gains are increases in economic benefits and are no different in nature from revenue. |

#### Typical account titles used

- **Service Income** — revenue earned by performing services for a customer or client, e.g. accounting services by a CPA firm, haircut services by a salon, construction services by an engineering firm.
- **Sales** — revenue earned from the sale of merchandise, e.g. a sale of furniture by a firm whose main line is manufacturing furniture.

### Owner's equity

- Also known as **net assets** or **equity**, capital refers to what is left to the owners after all liabilities are settled.
  - **Capital** — records the original and additional investments of the owner. It increases with profit earned during the year and decreases with a loss. Cash or other assets the owner withdraws ultimately reduce it. This account bears the name of the owner.
  - **Withdrawals** — when the owner withdraws cash or other assets, they are recorded in the drawing or withdrawal account rather than directly reducing the owner's equity account.
  - **Income Summary** — a temporary account used at the end of the accounting period to close income and expenses. It shows the profit or loss for the period before closing to the capital account.
